Project Write-Up

Design rationale and development overview for the Western US Wildfire Intensity & Land Surface Temperature interactive visualization.

Design Rationale

Research Question & Data Framing

The central question is whether wildfire hotspots in the western US have expanded geographically between 2018 and 2024, and whether land surface temperature warming predicts where new fire risk emerges. The two quantities needed to be visually separable yet comparable at the same resolution.

Spatial Unit: 1°×1° Grid Cells

The underlying data comes from NOAA GOES-16 geostationary satellite products (ABI-L2-FDCC for fire, ABI-L2-LSTC for land surface temperature). Both products are aggregated to a 1°×1° grid (~111 km per side at mid-latitudes) to reduce file size and align the two variables on a common spatial basis.

We considered rendering cells as a raster image (canvas pixels) for performance, but chose SVG paths instead because individual cell interactivity (hover tooltips, click-to-pin) required event targeting on discrete elements. SVG also scales correctly at all zoom levels without re-rendering.

A small inset (6% on each edge) was applied so cells render at 88% of their nominal size. This creates visible separation between adjacent cells, making spatial clustering of fire or heat signals immediately readable even without zooming.

Fire Intensity Encoding (FRP)

Fire Radiative Power spans roughly three to four orders of magnitude in this dataset — from near-threshold low-intensity burns (~5 MW) to peak seasonal totals around 21,000 MW in the most catastrophic cells. A sequential log-scale colormap (yellow → orange → deep red, from D3's YlOrRd) was chosen for three reasons:

  • A linear scale would compress nearly all variation into the low end, making moderate fire years visually indistinguishable from fire-free years.
  • The log scale preserves perceptual distance across the full intensity range, so a 10× difference in FRP looks like a consistent visual step regardless of where on the scale it occurs.
  • Yellow-orange-red is culturally associated with fire and heat, reducing the cognitive load of decoding the legend.

Cells below 5 MW FRP are rendered fully transparent rather than assigned a color, preventing low-noise detections from adding visual clutter to years with limited fire activity.

Land Surface Temperature Encoding (LST Anomaly)

Rather than showing absolute temperatures, we encode the deviation from each cell's own 2018–2024 summer mean. Absolute LST is dominated by latitude and elevation gradients that would make the desert Southwest perpetually red and the northern Rockies perpetually blue regardless of year to year variation. Anomalies reveal change, which locations are warming faster than their own baseline, making the map directly relevant to fire risk rather than simply reflecting geography.

A diverging blue–white–red colormap centered at zero was used. Blue encodes cooler-than-average conditions (which suppress fire risk); red encodes warmer-than-average (which amplify it). White at zero provides a clear neutral anchor. The color direction intentionally matches cultural conventions for cool and warm, and the diverging structure draws attention to the most extreme departures in either direction.

Layer Opacity & Linked Sliders

Because FRP and LST cells share the same spatial grid, one layer can fully obscure the other. The opacity control system allows users to balance visibility: default state shows FRP at full opacity and LST at 20% (LST provides background context). The inverse link (🔒) means boosting one layer automatically dims the other, maintaining a roughly constant total visual density. Users can unlock the sliders to control layers independently for detailed comparison.

We considered hiding LST entirely when FRP is at full opacity, but found that the faint LST background at 20% opacity provides useful spatial context, e.g., seeing that a fire-free region still has anomalously warm surface temperatures.

Zoom & State Isolation

The western US spans roughly 25° of latitude and 25° of longitude. At full extent, individual 1° cells are small and dense. Double-clicking a state triggers a smooth zoom-to-fit that fills the map area with that state's bounding box. Surrounding states are dimmed (not hidden) to preserve geographic context while focusing attention. The sidebar expands to widen the sparkline chart, taking advantage of the available space.

We evaluated a fixed-zoom-level approach (e.g., always zoom to scale 4) but found it worked poorly across states with very different areas (Nevada vs. Washington). The fit-to-bounds approach gives each state the same visual footprint regardless of size.

Sparkline: Per-Cell Temporal Trend

Clicking a cell pins it and reveals a dual-axis sparkline showing FRP (orange bars, left axis) and LST anomaly (blue line, right axis) across all seven years. This chart addresses the visualization's core question at the cell level: is this location getting hotter and more fire-prone over time? The dual-axis design was preferred over two separate charts because the fire–temperature relationship is most readable when both signals are visually aligned along the same time axis.

Tour System

Two guided tour modes were implemented to lower the barrier to insight:

  • Global main tour — steps year by year from 2018–2024, zooming to the highest-FRP state and highest-LST state each year with curated contextual descriptions. Designed for first-time users or anyone wanting a narrative overview of the seven-year period.
  • State mini tour — when zoomed into a state, the same button runs a focused tour that cycles through only the years with fire activity in that state, pinning the peak FRP cell and peak LST anomaly cell in turn. Designed for users who want to explore a specific region in depth.

A separate nav tour (triggered by the ? button) provides a UI orientation walkthrough for new users, with animated demonstrations of the layer controls, year slider, and zoom behavior.

Visual Theme

A dark background was chosen because fire and heat colors (yellows, oranges, reds) have far greater perceptual contrast against dark surfaces than against white. A light background would require substantially different colormaps or reduced saturation to avoid visual clashing.

Development Process

Development Timeline & Time Estimate

Total estimated time: ~42 person-hours across the team

A rough breakdown by phase:

  • Data pipeline (~12 hrs) — downloading GOES-16 ABI-L2-FDCC and ABI-L2-LSTC products from AWS S3 open data, parsing NetCDF files, aggregating pixel-level detections to 1°×1° cells, computing seasonal FRP totals (Jun–Oct) and summer LST anomalies (Jun–Sep relative to the 2018–2024 mean), and serializing to a compact cells.json format suitable for client-side loading.
  • Core map rendering (~7 hrs) — setting up the D3 Albers projection fitted to the 11 western states, building the LST and FRP cell layers, implementing the ocean mask (even-odd fill rule to clip cells to land), and handling the initial zoom extent and pan constraints.
  • Zoom & interaction (~5 hrs) — implementing double-click zoom-to-state with sidebar expansion, the click-to-pin sparkline, state hover highlighting, and the drag-vs-click disambiguation logic.
  • Tour system (~12 hrs) — the global main tour and state mini-tour required the most iteration: designing the state machine, computing best-FRP and best-LST targets per year, writing curated year × state descriptions, and handling edge cases (states with no fire in some years, tour interruption via year slider).
  • Nav tour & UI polish (~6 hrs) — the guided UI walkthrough with animated demonstrations of layer opacity, year scrubbing, zoom, and the mini-tour; the initial modal; positioning logic for pop-up arrows; and general CSS polish across all states and breakpoints.

What Took the Most Time

The data pipeline was the single most time-intensive phase. GOES-16 produces one file every ~10 minutes; a full fire season (Jun–Oct) across seven years is roughly 30,000 files. Filtering, deduplicating, and aggregating those at the right spatial resolution required careful attention to projection (the satellite uses a fixed-grid scan angle coordinate system, not lat/lon) and to the quality flag fields that distinguish actual fire pixels from false positives.

The zoom–sidebar interaction was the most technically tricky front-end problem. The sidebar expands via a CSS width transition (750ms), which changes the SVG's available width, which invalidates any zoom transform calculated before the transition completed. The final solution suppresses projection rebuilds during the transition and applies a corrective zoom-to-fit once the transitionend event fires.

Writing curated tour descriptions took longer than expected. We wanted to give each year × state combination in the tour a short, accurate, contextually meaningful narrative, not just data summaries. We aimed for causal explanations (heat domes, pyroconvection, El Niño/La Niña influence on fire seasons, etc.). This wasn't fully realized in this first version, but we wanted to set the foundation for potential future iterations to build on with more detailed descriptions.
